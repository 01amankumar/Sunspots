How this audit was actually done

Coverage is honest, not exhaustive: some entries rest on search-snippet summaries rather than full text (Solar Physics/ApJ/MNRAS are mostly paywalled), and I could not reach 20–30 papers with the depth the brief asked for in one pass — I'd rather hand you 19 real, checkable entries than pad to 30 with guesses. One finding changes the whole picture up front: your own 2025 Solar Physics paper (Kumar & Kumar, "Hybrid-Ensemble Deep-Learning Models...") is itself part of the prior-art set the next paper has to clear — and the field around hemispheric Cycle 25/26 prediction is far more crowded than the recursive-forecast work assumed. The good news: the search also surfaced one question nobody has actually asked yet.

Part B

Top 10 research gaps

1. Hemisphere-specific empirical predictability (forecast-skill decay vs. horizon)

★★★★★
Existing literature
Chen et al. 2024 predicts both hemispheres but reports only point estimates, no skill-vs-horizon curve. Phase-dependent predictability (ApJ aad197) exists only for the total signal, physics-based not ML.
What's been done
Hemisphere amplitude/timing asymmetry: extensively (Javaraiah I–V, Chen et al.). Total-signal predictability-vs-phase: yes, physics-based.
What's missing
Nobody has computed and statistically compared forecast-skill decay curves (RMSE/R² vs. horizon) separately for N and S, empirically, with ML, using a long record.
Why it matters
If the hemispheres are differently predictable, that's new evidence about asymmetric dynamo behavior — independent of whether any specific Cycle 26 number is later confirmed right or wrong.
Can your dataset address it?
Yes — the 151-year splice is exactly what's needed for stable long-horizon backtesting per hemisphere.
Can your current model address it?
Mostly yes — you already have validated direct-horizon ResidualLSTM and the failed-recursive backtest as a methodological control.
Additional experiment required
Horizon-stepped backtests (1, 3, 6, 12, 24, 36, 60, 120, 157 mo.) per hemisphere, with a formal statistical test (e.g. Diebold-Mariano or bootstrap) for N vs. S skill difference at each horizon.
Expected publication strength
Strong for Solar Physics / Space Weather if the difference is real and statistically supported either way (a null result is still publishable here).
Risk level
Moderate — result could be "no significant difference," which is still a valid, publishable finding but less exciting.
Genuinely new?
Yes, based on this search. The pieces (hemispheric DL, predictability-vs-phase) exist separately; the combination doesn't appear to.

2. Bridging empirical ML predictability to theoretical chaos limits, per hemisphere

★★★★
Existing literature
Lyapunov-exponent studies give a ~16-month theoretical predictability horizon for the total signal (multiple papers, 2007–2014).
What's been done
Chaos/Lyapunov estimation for total SSN and sunspot area.
What's missing
No Lyapunov/chaos estimate has been computed separately for N and S; no paper connects an empirical ML predictability horizon to the theoretical chaos-based one.
Why it matters
Would test whether one hemisphere is "more chaotic" than the other — a genuinely physical question, not just a modeling exercise.
Can your dataset address it?
Yes, same 151-year hemispheric series supports a per-hemisphere Lyapunov estimate (e.g. Wolf/Rosenstein algorithm).
Can your current model address it?
Partially — the ML predictability-horizon half is covered by Gap 1; the Lyapunov half needs a separate (well-established, off-the-shelf) nonlinear-dynamics computation.
Additional experiment
Compute largest Lyapunov exponent per hemisphere; compare to the empirical ML skill-decay horizon from Gap 1.
Expected strength
Strong if combined with Gap 1 as one paper; weak alone (a Lyapunov-exponent-only paper without the ML component is a much thinner contribution).
Risk
Chaos-metric estimates on this kind of noisy real-world series are sensitive to method choice — needs care and probably a sensitivity check across estimators.
Genuinely new?
Yes as a hemisphere-resolved, ML-connected analysis; the underlying chaos-estimation technique itself is old (not novel on its own).

3. Rigorous recursive-vs-direct forecasting comparison as a methodological study

★★★
Existing literature
General time-series ML literature has this debate (exposure bias, DeepAR, seq2seq direct-multi-output work), but I found no solar-physics paper that runs and reports both failure and success modes side by side for sunspot data.
What's been done
Most solar-cycle DL papers report only their chosen method's result, not a documented negative control.
What's missing
A solar-physics-specific, quantitative demonstration (which you already have, informally) of recursive collapse vs. direct-horizon stability.
Why it matters
Prevents the field from repeating the recursive-forecast mistake; useful as a cautionary methodological reference.
Can your dataset/model address it?
Yes — you already ran both and have the numbers (recursive R²=−0.61/−4.11; direct R² still weak but not degenerate).
Additional experiment
Formalize with more seeds/lookback variants; report as a clean ablation table rather than an aside.
Expected strength
Not strong as a standalone contribution — this is methodology hygiene, valuable as a section within a larger paper (e.g., inside Gap 1's paper), not a paper by itself.
Risk
Low technically, but a reviewer may correctly say "this is expected, not novel" if presented alone — matches the user's own concern about architecture-level novelty.
Genuinely new?
The specific empirical demonstration for hemispheric solar data: probably yes. The general phenomenon: well known in ML, not new to the field at large.

4. Joint multivariate North+South forecasting vs. independent models

★★★
Existing literature
Chen et al. 2024 model hemispheres, but it's unclear from available abstracts whether they explicitly test independent-vs-joint modeling as an ablation.
What's been done
Both hemispheres predicted, generally in the same paper/model, but a controlled independent-vs-joint ablation isn't clearly documented anywhere I found.
What's missing
A clean experiment isolating whether cross-hemispheric information transfer helps, at multiple horizons.
Why it matters
Tests whether the hemispheres are dynamically coupled enough that one helps predict the other — relevant to dynamo-coupling physics.
Can your dataset/model address it?
Yes — straightforward extension: a 2-output ResidualLSTM variant vs. two independent ones, same data.
Additional experiment
Train North-only, South-only, and joint [N,S]→[N,S] models; compare per-hemisphere skill.
Expected strength
Moderate as a standalone claim; strong as a secondary finding inside Gap 1's paper.
Risk
Could easily show "no meaningful difference," which is a fine but modest result.
Genuinely new?
Likely yes as a controlled ablation; the general idea of "hemispheres are coupled" is old physics (see Gap on polar flux imbalance), not new.

5. Cycle-phase-aware conditioning, tested rigorously

★★
Existing literature
You already tried a fixed 132-month sinusoidal phase feature on the (dropped) monthly-mean total series — it made results slightly worse.
What's been done
Phase-dependent predictability is established (physics-based, total signal). Explicit phase-as-ML-input has apparently not been tried for hemispheric data.
What's missing
A physically correct phase feature (using actual historical minima dates, not a fixed period) has not been tested, hemisphere-specific.
Why it matters
Could explain part of Gap 1's predictability differences mechanistically.
Can your dataset/model address it?
Yes, with better phase-feature engineering than the fixed-132-month attempt.
Additional experiment
True phase-since-last-minimum feature (piecewise from documented cycle boundaries), tested as an ablation within Gap 1's framework.
Expected strength
Weak alone (an ablation, not a paper); useful as a component/explanatory analysis for Gap 1.
Risk
You already have one negative result here (fixed-period phase feature hurt performance) — a second failure would be an honest, if modest, finding.
Genuinely new?
Marginal — this is architecture-level tinkering, the exact thing the brief said to reject unless it answers a genuinely new question.

6. Systematic hindcast across all 14 available cycles (12–25), hemisphere-resolved

★★★
Existing literature
Leave-one-cycle-out hindcasting exists for the total signal ("outperforms baseline 75% of the time," unspecified source paper). Javaraiah's series does per-cycle scalar prediction, not full-trajectory hindcasts.
What's been done
Total-signal hindcasting: yes. Hemisphere-resolved, full-trajectory hindcasting across many historical cycles: not found.
What's missing
A systematic "what would this model have said, deployed at each past cycle's minimum" study, separately for N and S.
Why it matters
Directly tests real-world forecast reliability without waiting a decade for Cycle 26 to resolve — satisfies the "not dependent on Cycle 26 finishing" requirement.
Can your dataset/model address it?
Yes — 151 years covers ~13 complete cycles, enough for a real (if small-N) hindcast study.
Additional experiment
For each of cycles 15–24 (leaving enough prior history to train), truncate data at that cycle's start, forecast it, score against the real outcome.
Expected strength
Strong as a companion to Gap 1 — converts "predictability" from an abstract backtest metric into concrete "here's what we'd have told you in 1954" case studies.
Risk
Small-N (only ~10 usable hindcast cycles) limits statistical power; early cycles have poorer underlying data quality.
Genuinely new?
The hemisphere-resolved, long-record version: yes, as far as this search found.

7. Joint amplitude + timing multi-task prediction, with N-S peak-time-difference as an explicit target

★★
Existing literature
Chen et al. report peak time and amplitude separately per hemisphere but as point outputs from a general forecast, not an explicit multi-task formulation with an N-S timing-difference target.
What's been done
Amplitude prediction, timing prediction: both common. Peak-time-difference as a distinct, directly-optimized target: not found.
What's missing
Explicit multi-task loss jointly predicting amplitude, timing, and their N-S differences.
Why it matters
Peak-time lag between hemispheres is a known, physically meaningful quantity (see the 2013 phase-lag paper) — forecasting it directly, rather than deriving it after the fact, could reduce compounded error.
Can your dataset/model address it?
Yes, with a small architecture change (extra output heads).
Additional experiment
Multi-task ResidualLSTM variant vs. deriving the same quantities post hoc from two independent forecasts.
Expected strength
Moderate — a nice methodological refinement, best as a secondary contribution.
Risk
Could show no benefit over post-hoc derivation, in which case it's a small negative result.
Genuinely new?
Marginal-to-moderate; closer to architecture-level than problem-level novelty.

8. Uncertainty-quantified hemispheric forecasting with horizon-dependent interval growth

★★★★
Existing literature
Bizzarri et al. 2022 do rigorous Bayesian uncertainty quantification — but for the total signal only, not hemispheric.
What's been done
Uncertainty-quantified DL forecasting: yes, for total SSN. Hemispheric: point estimates only (Chen et al., Ruiz et al. both report point forecasts or plain RMSE, not calibrated intervals).
What's missing
Calibrated, horizon-growing prediction intervals (conformal, quantile, or Bayesian) specifically for N and S separately.
Why it matters
A single point number ("peak = 47.0") is scientifically weaker than "peak = 47 [32, 61] with 90% coverage" — and lets you honestly report the wide uncertainty your own backtest already revealed (negative R² at long horizon) instead of a bare point estimate.
Can your dataset/model address it?
Yes — deep ensembles (you already have 3-seed ensembles) give a cheap uncertainty estimate; conformal calibration on top is straightforward.
Additional experiment
Quantile-loss or conformal-calibrated version of the direct multi-horizon model; report PICP (interval coverage) at each horizon, per hemisphere.
Expected strength
Strong when combined with Gap 1 — "here's how uncertain each hemisphere's forecast is, and why" is a complete, defensible paper.
Risk
Low-moderate; conformal prediction is well-established machinery, main risk is calibration failing under the same distribution shift that hurts point forecasts.
Genuinely new?
Yes for the hemispheric case specifically.

Part E

Single strongest recommended novelty

Working title of the contribution

Hemispheric predictability asymmetry: are the Northern and Southern solar hemispheres equally forecastable, and does that gap change with solar-cycle phase? — the first empirical, ML-based, long-record (1874–2025) answer to a question the field has asked about amplitude and phase, but not about forecast skill itself.

This is Gap 1, carrying Gap 8 (uncertainty quantification) and Gap 6 (multi-cycle hindcast) as supporting pillars, with Gap 9 (physical correlate) as the discussion/interpretation section. It satisfies every constraint in your Task 8: scientifically meaningful, confirmed-underexplored by direct search, feasible with data and code you already have, falsifiable, reproducible, doesn't require waiting for Cycle 26 to resolve, and produces quantitative, tabulable results (skill-vs-horizon curves, per-hemisphere Lyapunov estimates, hindcast scorecards) rather than a single contestable point forecast.

Part F

Complete experimental design

Research question

Do the Northern and Southern solar hemispheres exhibit systematically different empirical forecast predictability (skill decay with horizon), and if so, does this predictability asymmetry itself vary with solar-cycle phase and correlate with the known amplitude/phase asymmetry?

Hypothesis

H1 (primary): There is a statistically significant difference between North and South in forecast skill (RMSE/R²) at one or more forecast horizons, beyond what's explained by sampling noise. H0 (null, and a fully acceptable publishable outcome): No significant difference exists at any tested horizon — the hemispheres are equally predictable despite differing in amplitude and phase.

Scientific motivation

Amplitude and phase asymmetry between hemispheres is well established and physically linked to independent, imperfectly-synchronized hemispheric dynamos. If the dynamos are truly semi-independent, there's no a priori reason their predictability should be identical — one hemisphere could be a "noisier" dynamical system than the other. This has never been measured.

Novel contribution

(1) First empirical, ML-based, long-record (151-year) quantification of hemisphere-specific forecast-skill decay vs. horizon. (2) First connection between that empirical decay and the theoretical chaos/Lyapunov-based predictability literature, computed per hemisphere. (3) A documented negative control (the recursive-forecast failure) establishing methodological ground truth other groups can build on. (4) Calibrated, horizon-growing uncertainty intervals per hemisphere, not previously reported for hemispheric SSN.

Dataset

The 151-year spliced hemispheric monthly and 13-month-smoothed series (Veronig et al. 2021, 1874–2020, + SILSO official 2020–2025), validated in the overlap period (this session: r=1.000 both hemispheres after the column-mapping fix, mean abs. difference <0.03 sunspots). Document the splice and validation explicitly as a methods subsection — it's a real, checkable contribution in its own right, distinct from the ML.

Input variables

Primary: lagged hemispheric SSN (lookback window, size swept as an ablation). Secondary/ablation only, not the headline model: cycle-phase-since-minimum feature (computed from documented historical minima, not a fixed period — fixing the flaw in the earlier fixed-132-month attempt).

Target variables

Per-hemisphere SSN at each of several fixed horizons (1, 3, 6, 12, 24, 36, 60, 120, 157 months), evaluated both as point forecasts and as calibrated intervals.

Baselines

Naive persistence; climatological mean (per-phase, since a plain unconditional mean is a weak baseline for a cyclical signal); the already-validated single-horizon ResidualLSTM/LSTM/GRU suite; the failed recursive multi-step model (kept explicitly as a documented negative control, not discarded).

Proposed model

Direct multi-horizon ResidualLSTM (already built), trained and evaluated separately for North and South, with a deep-ensemble (3+ seed) or conformal-calibrated uncertainty layer added on top.

Ablation experiments

  • Lookback length sweep (14, 60, 132 months) × each hemisphere.
  • Independent North/South models vs. joint [N,S]→[N,S] model (Gap 4).
  • With vs. without a true cycle-phase-since-minimum feature (Gap 5, correctly implemented this time).
  • Recursive vs. direct-horizon, kept as the documented negative control.

Validation strategy

Primary: the existing chronological 70/15/15 split. Secondary, required for Gap 6: rolling-origin hindcast anchored at each historical cycle's minimum (cycles ~15–24, as many as leave enough prior training history), scored against the real historical outcome for that cycle.

Statistical tests

Paired bootstrap (as already used for the monthly-mean GRU-vs-ResidualLSTM tie) or a Diebold-Mariano test on squared-error differences, North vs. South, at each horizon — the exact test needed to make H1/H0 a real statistical claim rather than "our number was bigger."

Uncertainty methodology

Deep-ensemble spread as a first uncertainty estimate (cheap, already partially built); conformal calibration on top for a formally valid coverage guarantee. Report PICP (prediction interval coverage probability) at each horizon, per hemisphere — if North's intervals are well-calibrated but South's aren't (or vice versa), that itself is evidence for H1.

Expected figures

Skill-vs-horizon curves (RMSE and R²), North and South overlaid, with confidence bands; hindcast scorecard (predicted vs. actual peak, per historical cycle, per hemisphere); uncertainty-interval-width-vs-horizon plot; Lyapunov-exponent comparison bar chart with theoretical vs. empirical predictability horizon marked; a phase-stratified error plot (rise/max/decline/min × hemisphere).

Expected tables

Full metrics per hemisphere per horizon per model variant; hindcast results table (predicted vs. actual, per cycle); statistical test results (p-values, effect sizes) for the N-vs-S skill comparison at each horizon; ablation results (lookback sweep, joint-vs-independent, phase-feature).

Evaluation metrics

RMSE, MAE, R² per horizon; PICP and mean interval width for uncertainty calibration; Diebold-Mariano statistic / bootstrap CI for the core N-vs-S comparison.

Failure criteria (what would falsify H1)

If, across all tested horizons, the bootstrap/DM test shows no statistically significant N-vs-S skill difference (confidence intervals overlapping zero at every horizon), H1 is rejected and H0 stands — report this as the finding, not as a failed experiment. This is explicitly designed so a null result is still a complete, honest, publishable paper, not a dead end.

Part G

Exact research questions and hypotheses

  1. RQ1: Does empirical forecast skill (RMSE/R²) differ significantly between North and South at any forecast horizon from 1 to 157 months? H1: yes, at ≥1 horizon. H0: no, at any horizon.
  2. RQ2: Does the direction/magnitude of any skill asymmetry change with solar-cycle phase (rise/max/decline/min)? H1: skill asymmetry is phase-dependent. H0: asymmetry (if any) is constant across phase.
  3. RQ3: Does the empirical ML predictability horizon (where skill degrades to climatology/persistence level) align with the theoretical Lyapunov-based horizon (~16 months for the total signal), separately per hemisphere? H1: empirical and theoretical horizons agree within a documented tolerance, per hemisphere. H0: they diverge substantially, suggesting either method is measuring something different from "true" predictability.
  4. RQ4: Does joint [N,S] modeling outperform independent per-hemisphere modeling at any horizon? H1: yes, indicating exploitable cross-hemispheric coupling. H0: no, hemispheres are effectively independent for forecasting purposes.
  5. RQ5: Does historical amplitude-asymmetry (per cycle) correlate with that cycle's hindcast-measured predictability asymmetry? H1: significant correlation. H0: none detectable given ~10 usable historical cycles.

Part I

Expected contribution statement

"Using a validated 151-year (1874–2025) hemispheric sunspot-number record — four times longer than prior deep-learning hemispheric studies — we provide the first empirical, statistically tested comparison of forecast predictability between the Northern and Southern solar hemispheres across forecast horizons from one month to thirteen years. We document a methodological negative control (recursive multi-step forecasting fails catastrophically; direct multi-horizon forecasting does not), connect our empirical predictability-horizon estimates to prior chaos-theoretic (Lyapunov-exponent) limits computed separately per hemisphere, and report calibrated, horizon-growing uncertainty intervals absent from prior hemispheric point-forecast studies. We test, rather than assume, whether this predictability asymmetry tracks the well-established amplitude and phase asymmetry between hemispheres."

Part J

Likely reviewer objections

1. "Hemispheric asymmetry has been studied for decades (Javaraiah et al.) — what's new here?"
Javaraiah's series predicts amplitude from asymmetry, cycle-by-cycle, via classical regression. This paper measures predictability itself — a different, unaddressed quantity — via ML skill-decay curves.
2. "Chen et al. (2024) already did hemispheric deep learning for Cycles 25/26."
Correct, and cited directly. Their hemispheric data covers 1992–2023 (32 years); this study uses 1874–2025 (151 years). More importantly, they report point forecasts only — no horizon-vs-skill curve, no N-vs-S predictability comparison, no uncertainty quantification. Those specific absences are this paper's contribution, not "we also did hemispheric DL."
3. "Only ~10–13 usable solar cycles is a very small sample for a statistical claim."
True, and stated explicitly as a limitation. The primary claim (RQ1, skill-vs-horizon within the standard train/val/test split) rests on hundreds of monthly test points, not cycle-count; only the hindcast (RQ5) and phase-correlation analyses are cycle-limited, and are presented with appropriately wide uncertainty, not overclaimed.
4. "Could the observed asymmetry just be a smoothing artifact from the 13-month running mean?"
Addressed directly: the study runs on both the smoothed and (if retained) monthly-mean series, and any asymmetry claim is required to hold on the less-smoothed series too before being reported as robust.
5. "Why should a solar physicist care about an ML backtest metric?"
Because it's tied, explicitly, to two things physicists already care about: the chaos-theoretic Lyapunov literature (RQ3) and the known amplitude/phase asymmetry (RQ5) — the ML result is the bridge, not the endpoint.
6. "A negative result (H0, no asymmetry) is unpublishable."
Not for this venue/framing: "hemispheres are equally predictable despite differing in amplitude and phase" is itself informative about how (a)symmetric the underlying dynamo really is, and is pre-registered as an acceptable outcome, not spun after the fact.

Part L

Do-not-claim list

Claims the evidence gathered here does not support. Using any of these will hand a reviewer familiar with the literature an easy rejection.

  • "First deep learning model for hemispheric sunspot prediction." False — Chen et al. 2024 and at least one 2023 ScienceDirect paper precede this.
  • "First study of North-South asymmetry in solar cycle prediction." False — Javaraiah's 5-paper series runs 2007–2021.
  • "First to predict Solar Cycle 26." False — at minimum Chen et al. (2024), Ruiz et al. (2024), your own Kumar & Kumar (2025), and an LSTM-FCN paper (2025) already do this.
  • "Our model outperforms Ruiz et al. / Chen et al." based on the current point-forecast comparison alone. Not established — different data spans, different validation protocols, and your own backtest already shows weak long-horizon skill (R²<0). A comparison is only fair once both use matched validation windows and the same horizon definition.
  • "We use more historical data" as the novelty, on its own. Explicitly rejected in your own brief, and correctly so — longer data is a precondition for the real claim (predictability asymmetry), not the claim itself.
  • "Novel hybrid/LSTM+CNN/Transformer architecture." Already rejected in the earlier round of this work (ResidualLSTM has direct prior art: DLinear, RevIN-LSTM hybrids, ARIMA-LSTM hybrids) — do not resurrect this framing here.
  • "We establish the predictability limit of solar activity." Overreach — the Lyapunov/chaos literature already estimates a theoretical limit for the total signal; this work tests whether the empirical ML result agrees with it, per hemisphere — a narrower, defensible claim.
  • Any claim about the accuracy of the actual Cycle 26 peak number stated with confidence not backed by the calibrated interval — report it as a point estimate with its (likely wide) uncertainty band, not as a prediction to be believed.
